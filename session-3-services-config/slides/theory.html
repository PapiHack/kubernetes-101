Problem 1

Pods are moving targets

You cannot hardcode a Pod IP. Ever.

Scale to 5 and two more IPs appear; scale down and they vanish. A Deployment with 3 replicas is 3 different IPs — which one would you even call? You need something stable in front of a moving set of Pods.

The Service

Service
The Service is a stable name and virtual IP that load-balances across every Pod matching its selector. Pods come and go; the Service IP and name never change. Two ports to keep straight: `port` is what the Service listens on, `targetPort` is what the container listens on — mixing them up is a Session 4 bug.

The Service, field by field

A Service names no Pod at all. It states a label query — whatever matches, now or in ten minutes, is a backend.
Service
Same object on both sides — walk a frame, then point at what it drew. apiVersion/kind (teal) says which API type, so the box on the right is a Service. metadata (violet) is the name, and this name is the thing a caller types — the next slide is entirely about it. spec (amber) is what you want: a ClusterIP, so one virtual IP that only works from inside, and the port mapping. The two frames inside spec are the two questions every Service answers. selector (pink) — which Pods? Not by name: by label. Point at the pink chips on the two Pods, then at the pink frame in the YAML; a third Pod with app: hello joins with no edit to this file, and that is the whole trick. ports (amber) — which port where? port is the Service's own door, targetPort is the container's. They differ here on purpose (80 → 5000) so the two never blur; mixing them up is the Session 4 bug. Note - port is a list item, same dash as the Pod's containers list on Session 2 slide 9. Two things they will get wrong if you do not say them: a selector that matches nothing is accepted silently — no error, just zero endpoints and a connection refused, which is what kubectl get endpoints hello-svc is for; and targetPort must be the port the app actually listens on, 5000 for our Flask image, not 80.

Services have names

backend                            # same namespace — all you need
backend.default                    # <service>.<namespace>
backend.default.svc.cluster.local  # the full name

Nothing to install. Name the Service well — that name is your endpoint.

DNS for Services and Pods
Trace the picture, do not list it. Inside default (violet) the frontend Pod dials backend:5000 — a bare hostname, no IP, no config — and the backend Service spreads that across its two Pods. Then the interesting hop: one of those backend Pods is the thing that needs the database, and the database lives in another namespace, so the arrow leaves the violet box and enters the teal one. That crossing is the whole slide: the moment you leave your namespace the short name stops working and you must say postgres.data. A namespace is a naming boundary before it is anything else — the Session 2 callback, one sentence is enough. Every Service gets its DNS record automatically: nothing to install, no service discovery to configure, no restart when Pods change. The three forms below are the same record at three levels of qualification; students only ever type the first two. In the capstone their frontend calls http://backend:5000 and it simply works — call it out when it happens. Worth saying out loud: nothing here stops a cross-namespace call, DNS resolves it happily. Blocking it is a NetworkPolicy job, which is Session 4.

Three ways to be reachable

They are not three alternatives — they are three layers. Ask for a LoadBalancer and you get a NodePort and a ClusterIP underneath it, whether you wanted them or not.
Service
Read the three columns left to right and watch the picture grow — that is the whole slide. Column 1: the arrow from outside stops at the wall. A ClusterIP is a virtual IP that exists only inside the cluster, so the only thing that can call it is another Pod; this is what you want for a database or a back-end API, and it is what you get by default when you write no `type:` at all. Column 2 adds one thing: the same Service also opens the same high-numbered port on every node, so `<any-node-ip>:30080` now reaches it. Column 3 adds one more: your cloud provider creates a real load balancer outside the cluster, gives it a public IP, and points it at those node ports. Land the callout hard — students reliably think these are three alternatives to choose between. They are layers: a LoadBalancer Service still has a node port and still has a ClusterIP, and `kubectl get svc` shows all of it. On kind there is no cloud, so a LoadBalancer Service sits at `EXTERNAL-IP: <pending>` forever — worth demoing for ten seconds so they recognise it later. NodePort is the one they use today.

NodePort, concretely

Fine for learning, terrible for production: one arbitrary high port per service, no TLS, no routing.
One request, three ports, three colours — read the tags in the YAML, then follow the same colours down the right-hand side. nodePort 30080 (amber) is the door in the side of the node; the request arrives there. port 80 (pink) is the Service’s own port, what it answers on once you are inside. targetPort 5000 (teal) is the port the container is actually listening on — our Flask image. Three numbers, three different things, and a NodePort Service is the only place you see all three at once; that is why this slide exists. Two facts to say out loud. (1) Every node opens the node port, not just the one running the Pod — hit any node IP on 30080 and kube-proxy forwards it, which is why the picture draws the Pod inside the node but the chip says this node is not special. (2) curl http://localhost:30080 works from the laptop only because docs/kind-config.yaml maps host 30080 onto the kind node container — on a real cluster you would use a node’s IP. Valid nodePort range is 30000–32767; omit the field and Kubernetes picks one for you, which is why Lab 1 pins it. Real clusters use an Ingress or a LoadBalancer instead — next slide.

LoadBalancer, concretely

No cloud, no load balancer. On kind or k3d EXTERNAL-IP stays <pending> for ever — the node port still works, so you fall back to localhost:30080.
Service
One line changed from the last slide — type: LoadBalancer — and four things now exist. Walk the picture top down. The cloud load balancer (violet) is built by your cloud provider when you apply this file; it is not a Kubernetes object running in the cluster, which is why the box sits outside the dashed line and why there is nothing to install. Its address is what kubectl get svc finally shows under EXTERNAL-IP. It spreads traffic across every node’s node port (amber) — the same 30000–32767 mechanism as the previous slide, except you did not write the number: Kubernetes allocated it, which is what the greyed ghost line in the editor is showing. From there the one cluster-wide ClusterIP Service (pink) takes over; draw their eye along the band crossing both nodes and say it out loud, because the commonest wrong mental model is one Service per node. Then the selector picks the Pods and the container port (teal) ends the journey. The payload of the slide: a LoadBalancer Service contains a NodePort Service, which contains a ClusterIP Service — exactly the three-column picture from the Service types slide, now shown as one continuous path. That is why the node-port chip is the same amber chip here, there and on the NodePort slide. Finally, the honest caveat: on kind or k3d nobody creates the external load balancer, so EXTERNAL-IP hangs at <pending> — the Service is not broken, you just reach it the NodePort way. Say the cost too: one cloud load balancer per Service, each one billed, which is the argument for an Ingress on the next slide.

Ingress, in one picture

No controller installed → your Ingress does nothing at all. A classic first surprise.
Ingress
The same file on both sides — walk a frame, then the thing it draws. Amber, ingressClassName: which controller should pick this up. That one line is the whole relationship between the object and the software — the Ingress is a piece of YAML sitting in etcd, and a controller is a Pod running in the cluster that watches for Ingresses addressed to it and reconfigures itself. Nothing happens without one, which is the callout and the classic first surprise: kubectl get ingress shows your object, ADDRESS stays empty for ever, and there is no error anywhere. Then the two routes, violet and teal — point at a frame in the YAML and then at the box it produced. Both match the same host, and the path decides which Service gets the request. Say what this replaces: the previous two slides gave every app its own port or its own cloud load balancer; here one host, one IP, one certificate fronts the whole shop, and the Services behind it go back to being plain ClusterIP — nothing externally reachable at all. Two details worth ten seconds each. pathType: Prefix is required and people forget it; / as a Prefix matches everything that is not matched more specifically, so /api wins for its own traffic. And the port here is the Service’s port, not the container’s — 80 for the frontend, 5000 for our Flask backend, exactly the port field from the Service slide. We are not configuring one today; Session 4 has it as a bonus lab.

…or by hostname

These two rules can just as well live in two separate Ingress objects, in two namespaces. The controller merges everything addressed to it — that is how one entry point serves many teams.
Ingress
Same manifest shape as the previous slide, one level up: there the two rules differed by path, here they differ by host, and everything else is identical. The picture is the point and it has a shape worth naming — converge, then diverge. Both names are A records pointing at the same address, so the packets are indistinguishable at the network level; they arrive at the same controller on the same port with the same certificate. The only thing that separates them is the Host: header the browser sent, which is plain HTTP, older than Kubernetes, and exactly how shared web hosting has always worked. Demo the curl at the bottom if you have a cluster up — forging the Host header and reaching a different app from the same IP is the moment it clicks, and it also shows that the hostname is not a security boundary: anyone can send any Host header. Then the callout, which is the real-world shape: each team writes its own Ingress in its own namespace, all with the same ingressClassName, and the controller merges them. Two warnings worth naming. TLS needs either a wildcard certificate for *.example.com or one tls: block per host — the certificate is chosen by SNI, before the Host header is even read. And if two Ingresses claim the same host and path, the behaviour is not defined by Kubernetes; ingress-nginx gives it to the oldest object and logs a warning you will never see. Keep this slide to two minutes.
Perspective

Ingress is done evolving. Meet Gateway API.

Gateway API concepts · gateway-api.sigs.k8s.io
Ninety seconds, no lab, and the picture on the left is the whole argument. Ingress put one team’s concern and another team’s concern in one object: the platform team owns the TLS certificate and the public address, the app team owns the paths, and both edit the same YAML. Gateway API cuts that into three, and the colours follow the owner — amber GatewayClass is the implementation, chosen once by whoever runs the infrastructure; violet Gateway is the listener, the ports and the certificates, owned by the cluster operator; teal Routes are the rules, owned by the app team, and they attach themselves upward to a Gateway rather than being granted access downward. Say that last part plainly, because it is the design decision: the operator decides which namespaces may attach, the developer decides whether to, and neither can force the other. Then the two panels. The red one is why Ingress stopped: every feature anyone wanted — rewrites, canary weights, gRPC, header matching — arrived as an annotation like nginx.ingress.kubernetes.io/…, which means your manifest only works on one controller and silently does nothing on another. The green one is what you get instead, and weights is the one to name: a canary release is a field in the spec, portable across implementations. Two honest caveats. HTTPRoute and GRPCRoute are GA; TLSRoute, TCPRoute and UDPRoute are still experimental, so the TLSRoute in the picture is drawn as the docs draw it, not as something to build on today. And Ingress is not deprecated — it is everywhere, it works, it is simply finished. If they inherit a cluster next month it will have Ingress; if they design a platform next year, evaluate this first.

ConfigMap · Secret

build once, configure at run time

A password inside an image is readable by anyone who pulls it.

Same app, three environments, three database URLs. If you rebuild per environment you are no longer shipping the same tested artifact — that is the real argument, beyond convenience. Kubernetes gives you two objects for this: ConfigMap for non-sensitive values, Secret for the rest.

ConfigMap → into the container

Two doors into the same container. Keys become variables — or keys become filenames: ca.crt turns up as the file /etc/ssl/ca/ca.crt.
ConfigMaps
The same two objects on both sides, matched by colour — walk a frame on the left, then the frame on the right that consumes it. Teal, hello-env: plain key/value pairs, and envFrom pulls every key in as an environment variable, so the app reads GREETING with no code change. Use env: with configMapKeyRef instead when you want one key, or want to rename it. Violet, hello-ca: one key whose value is a whole file — note the |, which is YAML for "keep the line breaks", and the certificate is truncated here only to fit. It takes two blocks to use it, and that is the thing they get wrong: volumes at Pod level says which ConfigMap, volumeMounts inside the container says where it lands, and the name: ca-bundle in both is what ties them together — point at both lines. The rule for the mount: each key becomes a file named after the key, so ca.crt appears at /etc/ssl/ca/ca.crt. Say why you would ever want the file form: anything you are containerising that insists on reading nginx.conf, application.yaml or a CA bundle off disk. Two warnings worth 15 seconds each — a mountPath onto an existing directory hides what was there, and the env-var form is read once at start-up, which is exactly the next slide.

Secret — and an honest warning

A Secret keeps values out of your image and out of git. It does not make them safe — that is RBAC, encryption at rest, and an external manager.
Secrets
Two files on the left, one Secret. Amber, data: — values must already be base64, so you encode by hand and the file is unreadable to a human. Teal, stringData: — you write the value in clear and the API server encodes it for you on the way in. Same object afterwards: stringData is write-only, it never comes back when you read the Secret, you always get data. So use stringData for anything you type yourself; that is what the lab does. On the right, one environment variable, and it is env: + valueFrom + secretKeyRef rather than the envFrom of the previous slide — with secrets you name the one key you want, deliberately. The violet hello-secret appearing in all three files is the whole wiring; point at the three lines. Then the red band, and do it live rather than reading it: kubectl get secret hello-secret -o yaml, pipe the value through base64 -d, and the password is on screen. base64 is encoding, not encryption — it exists so binary values survive YAML, nothing more. What a Secret actually buys you is that the value is out of the image and out of git; what makes it safe is RBAC on who may read Secrets, encryption at rest in etcd, and an external manager such as Vault or a cloud KMS — Session 4 points there. Last thing, 10 seconds: an env var is visible to anything that can read the process environment, so the file mount from the previous slide is the better shape for a certificate or a long credential.

Probes: what “ready” actually means

Without a readiness probe, a rolling update happily sends traffic to Pods that aren't ready yet. Probes are what make it genuinely zero-downtime.
Configure probes · Pod lifecycle
Until now Kubernetes only knows whether the process is alive — not whether the app works. Three probes, three different questions, and the picture on the right is about when each one runs. startupProbe (amber) runs first and alone: while it is failing the other two are held, so a slow starter cannot be killed by its own liveness probe before it has finished booting. Its budget is failureThreshold × periodSeconds — 30 × 2s here, a full minute — and the moment it passes once it never runs again. That is the field people do not know exists; before it, the only tool was a long initialDelaySeconds on liveness, which is a guess you pay for on every restart. Then the two steady-state probes start, side by side and independent. readinessProbe (pink) gates Service membership — the callback to the label/selector picture from the first half — and failing it removes the Pod from the pool without touching it. livenessProbe (teal) is the restart-me signal. The one sentence to leave them with: only liveness restarts anything. The next two slides take each one apart, so keep this one to the shape and the timing.

Liveness: am I stuck?

A restart, not a reschedule. If the container keeps failing you get CrashLoopBackOff and the restarts slow down — 10s, 20s, 40s, up to five minutes.
Configure probes
Liveness answers one question and it is not "does my app work" — it is "is this process wedged in a way only a restart will fix". Walk the right-hand column top down: the kubelet, which lives on the node, not in the control plane, polls every periodSeconds; two successes; then the app deadlocks and the probe times out. Note it does not act on the first failure — failureThreshold: 3 is three consecutive misses, which with a 10s period is 30 seconds of being wrong before anything happens. Then the kubelet kills the container and starts a new one. Land the distinction hard, because it is the one they get wrong: the Pod is not replaced. Same name, same IP, same node, the AGE column keeps counting; only RESTARTS moves. A Deployment is what replaces Pods; liveness only restarts containers. Then the red box on the left: the most common production outage caused by a probe is a liveness probe that checks the database. The database gets slow, every Pod's probe fails at once, every container restarts at once, and now nothing is serving. Probe your own process — a handler that returns 200 if the event loop is turning, nothing more. If what you mean is "do not send me traffic while the database is down", that is a readiness probe, which is the next slide.

Readiness: send me traffic?

This is what makes a rolling update genuinely zero-downtime: a new Pod joins the Service only once it says it is ready, and an old one leaves before it is torn down.
Service · Pod lifecycle
This is the slide where the Service from the first half of the session comes back. The selector still matches all three Pods — the labels have not changed — but a Pod only enters the Service's endpoint list once its readiness probe passes. Point at hello-2: it is Running, zero restarts, nothing is wrong with it as a process, it simply says "not yet" and the Service quietly stops routing to it. Show the two commands together, because together they are the whole lesson: kubectl get pods gives 0/1 Running — the READY column is the probe talking, which is why that column exists and why it is not the same as STATUS — and kubectl get endpoints hello-svc is where you see the IP has gone. Teach that second command; it is the first thing to run when a Service "doesn't work", and in Session 4 an empty endpoint list is the symptom of a selector typo, which the API never errors on. Good reasons for readiness to fail on purpose: still loading a cache, waiting on a migration, a dependency is down and you would rather serve nothing than errors. And it is reversible with nothing else happening — one successful probe and the IP is back in the list. Contrast with the previous slide in one sentence: liveness failing restarts the container, readiness failing only takes it out of the pool.

Tell the scheduler what you need

No requests = the scheduler is guessing.

Resource management for Pods and Containers
This goes in the Deployment's Pod template, right next to the probes. Requests are accounting (scheduling), limits are enforcement (cgroups — Session 1 callback). Session 4's broken-manifests lab includes absurd requests producing an eternal Pending — name that now so it lands later. Common starting point: set requests from observed usage, set a memory limit, and be careful with CPU limits (throttling).

Requests: how the scheduler picks a node

A node can be full by requests and idle in reality. Nothing in this picture is measured — it is all arithmetic on numbers you wrote.
Resource management for Pods and Containers
Scheduling is simpler than people expect: for each node, add up the requests of everything already assigned to it, subtract from allocatable, and see whether the new Pod's requests still fit. That is the whole filter. Two consequences to land. First, nothing here is measured — a node whose Pods request 4 CPU and use 0.1 is full as far as the scheduler is concerned, which is the next slide. Second, a Pod that fits nowhere is not an error: it sits in Pending for ever, quietly, and the only place it says why is kubectl describe pod under Events — 0/3 nodes are available: 3 Insufficient cpu. Teach that command here; Session 4's broken-manifests lab has exactly this bug with an absurd request, so name it now and it will land then. Mention allocatable is less than the machine's size — the kubelet and the OS keep a slice back — so a 4 CPU node does not offer 4 CPU. And the one that bites beginners: with no requests at all the scheduler assumes zero, cheerfully packs the node, and the first thing you notice is something being evicted at 3am.

Limits: throttled, or killed

Resource management for Pods and Containers
One sentence carries this slide: CPU is compressible, memory is not. Over the CPU limit the kernel simply hands the container less CPU each scheduling period — it keeps running, keeps passing its probes, and gets slower. Nothing in kubectl get pods changes, which is why CPU throttling is the thing people spend a week debugging as "the app is mysteriously slow"; it shows up in metrics, not in status. That is also why a CPU limit is the one to be careful with: a limit well above the request buys you very little and can cost you a lot of tail latency. Over the memory limit there is no such option — you cannot give a process less memory than it has already allocated, so the kernel's OOM killer kills the container, and the kubelet restarts it. Point at the STATUS column: OOMKilled, RESTARTS 1, and if it keeps happening, CrashLoopBackOff. Tie it back to the probes slides: liveness failing and OOMKilled both end in a restart, but one is Kubernetes deciding and the other is the kernel deciding, and only the second shows up as OOMKilled. Practical advice to close: always set a memory limit, set it from observed usage plus headroom, and when a container is OOMKilled do not reflexively raise the limit — find out whether it is a leak first.

Over-provisioned: paying for air

The opposite is just as bad: requests far below real usage over-pack the node, and the first symptom is something being evicted or OOMKilled at 3am.
Resource management · Resource monitoring
This is the slide that turns requests from a YAML chore into money. The number you write is a reservation held for the whole life of the Pod, used or not — so "cpu: 1 to be safe" on four Pods fills a four-CPU machine that is 96% idle, and the fifth Pod goes Pending with Insufficient cpu on a node you could run twenty of these on. In a cloud cluster that is where the autoscaler adds a node and the bill goes up for nothing. The fix is not clever: measure. kubectl top pod gives you current usage — say that it needs metrics-server installed, and that on kind you may have to install it, so it is a "know it exists" command today rather than a lab step; a real cluster has history in Prometheus and that is what you would actually size from. Then set the request near observed usage plus headroom, and the limit higher. Say the symmetric failure out loud too, because a room that has just been told "requests are waste" will go and delete them all: requests that are too small let the scheduler over-pack the node, and then your Pods fight for CPU and get OOMKilled under load. The goal is accurate, not small. Mention that VPA and tools like Goldilocks exist to recommend values from real usage, one sentence, no detail.

Bonus

Where the bytes actually live

Delete the PVC and, with the default reclaim policy on a dynamically provisioned volume, the disk goes too. The claim is what keeps the data alive.
Persistent Volumes · Storage Classes
The whole picture in one go, and the point is the bottom third: a PV is not storage, it is a record describing storage that exists somewhere else. Walk it. You write the PVC — "5Gi, ReadWriteOnce" — inside your namespace, next to your Pod. The StorageClass names a driver; that driver calls your cloud and creates a real disk. Kubernetes then writes a PV object describing it, and binds your claim to it. Your Pod names the claim, never the disk, which is why the same YAML works on kind, on AKS and on EKS with a different thing at the bottom each time. Three things worth saying out loud. The PVC is namespaced and the PV is not — the callback to Session 2's namespaced-vs-cluster-scoped slide, and the reason two teams cannot accidentally claim the same volume. In real life you almost never write a PV; the previous slide wrote one by hand so the two objects were both visible, but with a StorageClass you write the claim and the PV appears, which is why its name is an ugly generated pvc-8f3a1c…. And ReadWriteOnce means one node, because a cloud disk is attached to one machine at a time — that is physics, not a Kubernetes rule, and it is why the bonus lab runs a single replica. Close on the warn callout: the claim is the thing that owns the data's lifetime; delete it and a dynamically provisioned disk is deleted with it.
